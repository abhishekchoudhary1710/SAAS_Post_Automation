# LinkedIn competitor research for the Sarthi apps

Research date: 28 September 2026. Research and proposed experiments; not a deployment specification.

Implementation follow-up, same day: the owner authorized new visuals and videos. The first
rollout uses four PDFs, three edited explainer videos and seven images per week, with fresh
labelled website captures and fictional before/after lessons. Real screen-recorded walkthroughs
and the four-video mix discussed below remain later experiments. See
[current operations](LINKEDIN-GROWTH.md) and [asset provenance](LINKEDIN-ASSETS.md).

The most useful direction is to demonstrate one recognisable problem and one product action per post, then make the next step easy. Combine useful lessons with actual demonstrations and real people’s experiences. The existing Sarthi campaign has the lessons and direct links; its largest gaps are repetitive promotional copy, limited visual variety and little evidence from real product use.

## Scope and evidence limits

Reviewed six relevant product brands, their publicly accessible company pages, and 15 selected posts/articles listed below. Sources include brand posts, founders, users and third-party creators. Selection was purposive, not a random sample: relevant demonstrations, educational posts, recommendations and contrasting engagement examples. Some examples are historical; current company-page snapshots provide additional context.

LinkedIn exposes different public views to search and signed-out readers. Dates, follower counts and comment counts sometimes differed between snapshots. Counts below describe the retrieved view, not an audited live counter. Public reactions/comments do not establish impressions, website visits, purchases, paid promotion or causality. A missing count means unavailable, not zero. Recommendations about why a format might help Sarthi are hypotheses.

Formats are identified from accessible post text, transcripts and attachment labels. This is not a frame-by-frame audit of every video or slide. No private competitor analytics were available. No reliable competitor posting-time optimum or complete publishing-frequency comparison can be inferred from these partial feeds.

## What can plausibly help distribution and traffic

1. **Specific utility.** A reader can use an example even without buying. Teal’s formulas offer a useful pattern: make an improvement visible before asking for a click. Our hypothesis is that this gives readers more reason to finish, save or share the post.
2. **Visible product behaviour.** A real walkthrough answers what the tool actually does. Test input → action → output, with large enough interface detail to read on a phone. Creator demonstration examples above justify testing this, not assuming video will beat every PDF.
3. **People and communities.** Real accounts explain what they found useful. Where available, publish permissioned customer experiences; collaborate manually with relevant career educators or communities. Do not manufacture customer quotes or automatically contact people identified in this research.
4. **One relevant next step.** Link an interview lesson to the Prep demo, an application workflow to Apply, and a Windows live-assistance demonstration to Live. Competitors use several link placements; the sample does not establish that hiding links in comments improves reach. Preserve our direct tracked caption links.
5. **Useful responses.** Answer actual setup, pricing and product questions personally. Buffer’s observational research associates replies with stronger engagement, but does not prove replies cause growth. [Buffer engagement study](https://buffer.com/resources/state-of-social-media-engagement-2026/).

LinkedIn says it is reducing generic recycled content and engagement bait and acting against automated comments and engagement pods. Use the concrete examples above to improve substance, not to imitate prompts that solicit empty comments. [LinkedIn’s March 2026 feed update](https://news.linkedin.com/2026/ImprovingTheFeed).

## Audit of our current automation

Local sources: `knowledge/linkedin.json`, `agent/linkedin_growth.py`, `agent/publish/buffer.py`. Captions were composed locally from all 48 product topics on 28 September; whitespace-separated word counts include hashtags and the URL as tokens. No publication call was made for this audit.

| Product | Authored topics | Median caption words | Identical offer paragraph words | Median share of caption |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Prep | 20 | 136 | 55 | 40% |
| Apply | 12 | 121.5 | 47 | 39% |
| Live | 16 | 140 | 65 | 46% |

Our links, labelled examples, actual interface screenshots and practical lessons are useful foundations. The repeated offer occupies a large fraction of each caption. Every Live topic also uses the same underlying interface image with a changed heading, limiting visual variety.

Recommended changes, in order:

- Shorten the offer to the feature and entry point relevant to that post. Keep material setup requirements close to the invitation. Put the full price comparison in dedicated offer/FAQ posts and on the landing page. This is a copy test, not a universal word-count rule.
- Add original weak/strong example pairs to PDFs. Use hypothetical labels; never imply a fictional example is a customer’s result or actual app output.
- Introduce real walkthroughs. Existing LinkedIn code submits images and PDFs, so native video requires an implementation and publication test before scheduling. A script or animated mockup is not a recording of the working app.
- Obtain genuine customer evidence as it becomes available. A real support question can become a useful FAQ without publishing the customer’s identity.
- Revisit evergreen topics each month instead of treating a 28-day reuse interval as proof the material stays fresh.

Keep the existing products and daily schedule; first test individual changes, then consider this fuller mix. These are proposed assets, not files already produced or queued.

| Product | Weekly total | Proposed formats and subjects |
|---|---:|---|
| Prep | 5 | Two before/after answer PDFs; one practice-exercise PDF; one real practice-and-feedback video; one FAQ image |
| Apply | 3 | One Chrome walkthrough video; one annotated CV comparison image; one job-match checklist PDF |
| Live | 4 | Two actual test-call videos with different use situations; one setup image; one requirements/offer FAQ image |

Total: four PDFs, four videos and six images. Start by replacing only two existing slots with measured demonstration pilots after video support is ready. Founder content is an additional optional distribution activity requiring the owner’s real experience and voice. It should not be fabricated or automatically posted to the owner’s profile. Customer stories should replace a relevant product slot when real, approved evidence is available.

### A. Prep: make the improvement visible

Format: four-page PDF. Cover asks for a clear project explanation; slide two contrasts two explicitly hypothetical answers; slide three shows real feedback from a recorded test session, labelled as a demonstration; slide four invites the free demo. The actual test-session evidence still needs to be captured.

> Can you explain one project without listing every tool you used?
>
> Try this order: the problem, your contribution, one decision, and how you checked the result.
>
> Hypothetical example:
> “I worked on a booking API.”
> becomes
> “I added date validation to the booking API and tested invalid ranges before database writes.”
>
> Use details from your own work. Say the answer aloud and check whether your contribution is clear.
>
> Try a spoken mock interview with your CV in Prep Sarthi. The free 7-minute browser demo needs no card, account or API key.
> https://interviewsarthi.com/prep/

Format: a proposed 25–40-second real screen recording using fictional application data. Show an empty supported form, ApplySarthi filling it, then the user checking fields and the attachment. End before final submission. Duration is an editorial test, not an algorithm requirement.

> A filled application still needs your final check.

> In this demo, ApplySarthi enters the details in Chrome. Then we check the CV attachment, contact details and questions that need a personal answer.

> You review and submit the application yourself.

## Measurement plan

Hold posting frequency, timing, product destinations and prices steady during the first copy experiment. Compare a compact product paragraph against the existing paragraph within the same product and format. Alternate comparable slots across weeks so one version does not always receive the stronger day. This small organic test is directional, not a randomised causal study.

After the copy test, pilot demonstrations against the existing assets; avoid changing copy, format and time together. Record creative ID, product, format, hypothesis, planned time and actual sent time. Use the existing day-1 and day-7 snapshots for reach and engagement, marking unavailable values as unknown.

Evaluate tracked landing sessions and product starts alongside impressions. For Prep, compare demo starts and paid checkouts; for Live, downloads and checkouts; for Apply, useful free-product activity. Verify purchases against payment records. LinkedIn clicks are not equivalent to outbound website visits, and GA4 sessions divided by impressions are a directional ratio, not LinkedIn CTR.

Do not rank one product against another on raw sales: Apply is free and the three journeys differ. If numbers are too small, continue gathering evidence instead of declaring a winner. Review after four weeks, allowing the last week’s posts to reach comparable age. No automatic performance-based schedule changes are implemented by this research.

## Decision

Prioritise shorter, more specific captions and clearer examples first. Add real demonstrations next, then genuine customer stories when available. Keep the two-post daily schedule while measuring useful visits and product starts. The observed competitor posts support these tests; they do not establish a guaranteed reach or sales formula.
